# English Accounting module

Venues include *The Accounting Review, Journal of Accounting Research, Journal of Accounting and Economics, Review of Accounting Studies, Contemporary Accounting Research,* and *Journal of the American Taxation Association*. The patterns are distilled from roughly 255 accounting articles in the source library.

Reproduce the logic and register, never a corpus sentence.

## Register

Accounting writing is theory-anchored and carefully hedged. It names a construct and theoretical framework, derives a directional prediction, validates measurement, and interprets evidence in “consistent with” language when the design cannot isolate a unique channel. Prior literature is engaged closely.

## Abstract

A common abstract contains six to nine sentences:

1. State what the paper examines.
2. Give the theory or prediction that makes the relation interesting.
3. Describe the setting, research design, and measure.
4. Report the main directional finding.
5. Present cross-sectional or conditioning evidence.
6. Interpret the pattern relative to the theory and note real effects when supported.

Accounting abstracts should make construct measurement legible. Add JEL codes or keywords only when the requested format requires them.

## Introduction

The accounting introduction is literature-embedded and prediction-driven:

1. **Construct importance:** establish the reporting, disclosure, audit, tax, or control problem.
2. **Close gap:** explain what prior work does not resolve and why that omission matters.
3. **Question:** state what the study examines.
4. **Theory and prediction:** name the framework, derive the directional prediction, and acknowledge a genuine competing prediction.
5. **Setting and identification:** explain the shock, design, or validated measure and state the identifying assumption.
6. **Findings:** present the main and cross-sectional results with calibrated “consistent with” interpretation.
7. **Contribution:** explain the incremental contribution to the closest literature and any relevance to regulators or standard setters.

Do not create a “surprising gap,” a theoretical prediction, or a regulatory audience that the manuscript does not support.

## Section architecture

Typical empirical structure:

1. Introduction
2. Background and hypothesis development
3. Data, sample, and measurement
4. Research design
5. Main results
6. Additional and cross-sectional analyses
7. Robustness and alternative explanations
8. Conclusion

Hypotheses are usually explicit and theory-derived. Analytical papers instead move from model to propositions and testable implications.

## Results register

- Link each core finding to the corresponding prediction without claiming the test proves the mechanism.
- Report the coefficient and economic size, while emphasizing predicted sign and cross-sectional coherence.
- Treat construct validity and measure validation as central evidence.
- Do not infer that “more disclosure,” “more exclusions,” or a directional change in a proxy necessarily means greater transparency, reporting quality, or welfare without a validated construct link.
- Use cross-sectional tests to evaluate the channel, not as a decorative collection of splits.
- Address alternative measures, selection, reverse causality, and alternative explanations in a structured way.
- Connect reporting behavior to real effects only when the evidence supports that step.

## Contribution and positioning

Useful rhetorical functions include:

- identify the specific construct or theory the paper extends;
- explain how the measure, setting, or design addresses a limitation in prior work;
- distinguish a new mechanism from a new sample;
- state implications for regulators or standard setters cautiously.

Verify any “first study” or “answers a call” claim before using it.

## Referee lens

Review in this order:

1. **Construct and measurement validity:** is the key variable measured credibly and validated?
2. **Theory–prediction link:** does a named framework generate the hypotheses?
3. **Identification:** are endogeneity, reverse causality, omitted variables, and selection addressed?
4. **Cross-sectional coherence:** do conditioning tests actually discriminate the proposed mechanism?
5. **Contribution:** is the increment clear relative to specific work?

Deliver a summary, construct and measurement assessment, theory and identification assessment, cross-sectional assessment, contribution assessment, and minor comments.

## Register cues

Common, when accurate: “we examine whether,” “consistent with,” “we predict,” “prior research documents,” “cross-sectional variation,” “we validate our measure,” “real effects,” and “these findings should interest regulators.”

Avoid unearned causal verbs, a buried hypothesis, connector chains, and a contribution paragraph that merely repeats the results.
