# English Economics module

Venues include *American Economic Review, Quarterly Journal of Economics, Journal of Political Economy, Econometrica, Review of Economic Studies, Review of Economics and Statistics, AEJ: Applied, AEJ: Policy, Journal of Public Economics, Journal of International Economics,* and *Journal of Labor Economics*. The patterns are distilled from roughly 370 economics articles in the source library.

Reproduce the logic and register, never a corpus sentence.

## Register

Economics writing is question-driven and identification-centered. It opens with an important question rather than a bare literature gap, foregrounds the research design, and states a causal claim confidently only when the design supports it. Prose is spare; first person is standard; magnitudes and elasticities matter.

## Abstract

A common top-field abstract is one dense, question-led paragraph:

1. Open with the question or economic tension.
2. Name the setting, data, and source of identifying variation.
3. State the findings as committed claims, including signs and magnitudes.
4. Report a meaningful asymmetry, null, or heterogeneity when central.
5. Close with the mechanism, welfare implication, or economic upshot.

Use present-tense first person where natural. Do not add JEL codes or keywords unless the requested format calls for them.

## Introduction

Economics introductions are relatively short and front-loaded:

1. **Big question or motivating tension.**
2. **Why it matters and why it is difficult.** State the identification or data obstacle directly.
3. **What the paper does.** Make the design the star and explain the source of variation.
4. **Identification defense.** Address as-good-as-random assignment, selection, pre-trends, bunching, spillovers, and concurrent shocks as relevant.
5. **Results preview.** Give magnitudes and important heterogeneity. State bounded nulls rather than hiding them.
6. **Literature placement.** Position the paper against the question and closest work, usually after the results preview.
7. **Roadmap.** Use only when it helps navigation.

An epigraph or anecdote is optional, not a template requirement.

## Section architecture

Typical empirical structure:

1. Introduction
2. Setting, institutional background, or conceptual framework
3. Data
4. Empirical strategy and identification threats
5. Main results
6. Mechanisms and heterogeneity
7. Model or counterfactuals when applicable
8. Conclusion

Adapt this order to the paper. Do not force a short paper or theory paper into an empirical template.

## Results register

- Use causal verbs only when the design earns them.
- State elasticities, percentage-point changes, and welfare or counterfactual magnitudes plainly.
- Establish the first stage, balance, or source of identifying variation before interpreting the effect.
- Bound nulls: state what effect sizes the estimates rule out.
- Frame heterogeneity as economics and connect it to a mechanism or model.
- Distinguish the estimand from broader population claims, especially for LATEs.

## Contribution and positioning

Useful rhetorical functions include:

- explain how the findings speak to a long-standing question;
- compare the setting or design with the closest work;
- identify what can be learned because of the new variation or data;
- state policy implications as measured takeaways rather than advocacy.

Use “first evidence” language only after verifying the literature.

## Referee lens

Review in this order:

1. Is the question first-order and clear?
2. Is the design credible? Examine selection, pre-trends, spillovers/SUTVA, manipulation, concurrent shocks, and external validity.
3. Are magnitudes economically meaningful and mechanisms distinguished from stories?
4. Does causal language remain within the design and estimand?
5. Does the literature positioning clarify the question rather than pad citations?

Deliver a paper summary, identification assessment, magnitude and mechanism assessment, interpretation limits, then minor writing points.

## Register cues

Common, when accurate: “identifying variation,” “we exploit,” “quasi-exogenous,” “as good as random,” “pre-trends,” “bunching,” “rule out,” “on the margin,” “welfare implications,” “reduced-form,” and “structural.”

Avoid “is associated with” when making an earned causal claim, but never strengthen a merely associational design. Avoid connector chains, empty importance claims, and an introduction that merely expands the abstract.
