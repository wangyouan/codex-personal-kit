# Content and Citation Rules

## Narrative blueprint

### Title page

Keep only the paper title, authors, affiliation, conference, location, and date. Do not display the session name, track, room, or time unless the user asks for logistical details on the slide.

### Opening

Use a concrete fact, event, or news item when it makes the research question plausible. State its evidentiary role. A survey or newspaper story can motivate salience but does not become causal evidence about the paper’s outcome.

### Question and answer

Define the event or post period carefully. If several political and economic changes move together, call it a broader regime or geoeconomic break rather than a clean tariff coefficient.

Give the audience the answer before the technical design. Preview the important magnitudes and the limitation that most affects inference.

### Framework

Organize theory around a tension, not a literature inventory. When two forces compete, state:

- what raises the outcome;
- what attenuates it;
- which empirical boundary distinguishes the predictions.

### Design

Show the identifying group, not only the full sample. Separate:

- treatment construction;
- outcome construction;
- the most consequential measurement risk;
- the estimating equation and estimand.

### Evidence

Use claim-led titles such as “The Differential Appears in 2019, Not Before the Shock.” Put the magnitude and benchmark rate near the table or figure. State what the contrast makes less compelling and what it still cannot rule out.

### Closing

Resolve the opening question. Separate “supported by the estimates” from “not identified by the design.” End with the economic or governance implication, not a generic gratitude slide.

## Citation policy

### Keep fully visible

- News articles and quotations.
- Externally sourced images and charts.
- Institutional timelines when the source matters to credibility.
- Data-source attribution when the audience needs it to interpret construction.

### Integrate into the claim

Use one or two anchor references:

```latex
{\scriptsize\textcolor{xmuLink}{
Salient identities can shape leader evaluation
(Hogg, 2001; Park and Westphal, 2013).}}
```

or:

```latex
\item Standard errors clustered by firm, following Bertrand et al. (2004).
```

Avoid:

```latex
\slidecite{Related work: A; B; C; D; E.}
```

### Preserve the complete trail

Put full sources in notes:

```latex
\note{[Sources] Authors' manuscript, Table 2; full supporting references.}
```

Retain complete entries in appendix reference slides. Removing a visible citation inventory must not delete the underlying reference.

## Writing and visual rules

- One primary claim per slide.
- One table, figure, or conceptual composition per slide when possible.
- Use audience-facing prose, not production notes.
- Avoid repeated card grids and dashboard-like styling.
- Preserve comfortable margins and the existing visual system.
- Never shrink the title to solve body-density problems.
- Keep titles on one line when the template expects one line.
- Treat colored callout boxes as scarce emphasis.

## Claim calibration

- “Consistent with” for mechanism-compatible patterns.
- “Suggests” for evidence with material alternative explanations.
- “We find” for descriptive estimates.
- Causal verbs only when the design earns them.
- Do not infer intent, discrimination, welfare, or executive-specific capital from proxies that do not measure those constructs.

## Appendix

Use the appendix for:

- detailed variable definitions;
- alternative specifications;
- data construction tables;
- event-level validation priorities;
- comparison with closest papers;
- complete references and data sources.

Keep appendix slides readable enough to present during Q&A.
